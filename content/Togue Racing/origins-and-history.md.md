# Origins and History of Touge Racing
Over time, touge developed beyond informal mountain-road driving and became a recognizable part of Japanese automotive culture. During the 1980s and 1990s, Japanese performance cars, automotive magazines, racing videos, and enthusiast communities helped spread interest in mountain-pass driving. Vehicles such as the Toyota AE86 became strongly associated with this culture because of their lightweight construction, rear-wheel-drive layout, and connection to grassroots motorsports. The popularity of drifting also helped establish a distinct identity for Japanese performance driving. In 1995, the manga series Initial D began publication, introducing fictional mountain-road competitions inspired by real Japanese locations and automotive communities. Its popularity eventually helped introduce touge culture to international audiences. Video games, animated adaptations, and automotive publications further expanded its influence. Today, touge remains an important historical and cultural influence within the broader Japanese domestic market automotive scene.
## Introduction 
Touge (峠) is the Japanese word for a mountain pass, referring to a road or route that crosses mountainous terrain. Japan's geography contains extensive mountain ranges, making mountain roads an important part of transportation and regional travel. Over time, these roads also became associated with Japanese automotive culture. As automobile ownership increased during the postwar economic expansion, more people became interested in vehicle performance, driving skill, and motorsports. This helped create a grassroots interest in mountain-road driving that eventually became known as touge culture. Today, touge is recognized as an important part of Japanese car culture and is closely connected to the development of performance vehicles, drifting, and automotive media.

## Historical Development of Japanese Mountain Roads
Japanese mountain roads have a long history that predates the development of automobiles. Japan's mountainous geography made mountain passes important routes for transportation, trade, and communication between different regions. During the Edo period (1603–1867), roads such as the Tokaido connected major cities and supported travel throughout the country. One notable example was the Hakone mountain crossing, which presented difficult terrain for travelers. These historical routes later became part of the transportation network used by motor vehicles as Japan modernized.

## The Emergence of Touge Culture
The emergence of touge culture was closely connected to Japan's growing interest in automobiles and grassroots motorsports. During the 1960s and 1970s, increased car ownership and changing youth culture contributed to new forms of informal driving activity. Some enthusiasts began gathering around roads and other locations to demonstrate vehicle performance and driving ability. These individuals became associated with the term _hashiriya_, which describes street-racing enthusiasts. Mountain roads eventually became important locations within this broader automotive subculture because their winding layouts presented challenging driving conditions. By the 1980s, mountain-pass driving had become recognizable within Japanese performance-car culture. However, these activities were not always organized motorsports. Some involved illegal speeding on public roads, creating serious safety concerns for other drivers and surrounding communities. The development of touge culture therefore reflects both Japan's enthusiasm for automobiles and the complicated relationship between grassroots motorsports, public safety, and road regulations.

## The Influence of Japanese Motorsport
Japanese professional motorsports played an important role in the development of touge culture and drifting. During the 1970s, racing driver Kunimitsu Takahashi became known for using controlled oversteer to maintain speed through corners during circuit competitions. His driving style attracted attention from racing fans and influenced younger automotive enthusiasts. One of those influenced was Keiichi Tsuchiya, who later became widely known as the Drift King. Tsuchiya developed his reputation through both professional racing and his interest in mountain-road driving. In 1987, the release of the Pluspy video showcased his drifting abilities in a Toyota AE86 on the Usui mountain pass. The video helped introduce drifting to a much wider audience and strengthened the relationship between Japanese motorsports and grassroots automotive culture. These developments eventually helped drifting grow beyond informal driving communities into an internationally recognized motorsport discipline.

## The Development of Touge Into a Subculture
The development of touge into a recognizable automotive subculture was influenced by Japanese car manufacturers, motorsports, and popular media. During the 1980s and 1990s, Japanese performance vehicles became increasingly popular among driving enthusiasts. Cars such as the Toyota AE86 gained recognition for their lightweight construction and rear-wheel-drive design. Automotive magazines, racing videos, and enthusiast communities helped spread information about vehicle modifications and driving culture. A major turning point came in 1995 when the manga series Initial D began publication. Created by Shuichi Shigeno, the series introduced readers to mountain-pass racing through fictional locations inspired by real Japanese roads. Its later anime adaptation and related video games helped introduce Japanese automotive culture to audiences outside Japan. As a result, touge became more widely recognized as part of the international JDM community.
